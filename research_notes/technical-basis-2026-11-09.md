# Technical basis for Grapim's check (2026-11-09)

What the research says about how Grapim checks a customer-facing AI against the company's written policies, and what it should change. Every paper below was opened on arXiv or the official blog on 2026-11-09. Figures are quoted only as the source states them. Anything not confirmed on the page is marked **(unverified)**.

## How Grapim works today (from the code)

- `src/v1/risk.mjs`: an LLM pulls rules from the policy text, each with its clause and a quote. It writes `probes` single-turn customer messages per rule (quick: 8 rules x 2; full: 40 rules x 3). `callTargetFull` sends each message and captures `actions`. The judge sees the clause, the quote, the rule, the message, the answer, `actions_taken` and a keyword hint, and returns kept or broken.
- Re-asks happen **only when the first verdict is broken**. Each retry calls the agent again and judges again. With `TRIES=3`, the majority decides. If at least one try kept the rule, the probe is marked `unstable`, and a 1-of-3 break is recorded as **kept**.
- Overrides go in `overrides.json`. Every read applies them, and the judge's original verdict is kept as `judgeStatus`. Nobody computes agreement from this data yet.
- `src/v1/fix.mjs` drafts one block of instructions covering the broken rules, plus capability `gaps`. `recheck` re-runs **the same messages**.
- `src/v1/live.mjs` judges a **single exchange** (one message and one answer, with no earlier turns) against **every rule of the last scan in one LLM call**.

## Corrections to the brief

- Crescendo: the arXiv title is "Great, Now Write an Article About That: The Crescendo Multi-Turn LLM Jailbreak Attack". The authors are Russinovich, Salem and Eldan (Microsoft), first posted 2 Apr 2024. Any venue beyond arXiv is **(unverified)**.
- tau2-bench exists. Its authors are Barres, Dong, Ray, Si and Narasimhan (2025), not Yao. It is a separate paper, not a version 2 of tau-bench.
- Zheng et al.'s "over 80% agreement" applies to **pairwise preference** between chat answers, excluding ties. It is not a measure of binary policy verdicts. Don't cite it as evidence that Grapim's judge is accurate.

## The four papers you named

**1. tau-bench: A Benchmark for Tool-Agent-User Interaction in Real-World Domains.** Shunyu Yao, Noah Shinn, Pedram Razavi, Karthik Narasimhan (Sierra), arXiv 17 Jun 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045 . Blog: https://sierra.ai/blog/benchmarking-ai-agents (Narasimhan, 20 Jun 2024).
- Finding: an LLM plays the user over several turns (gpt-4-0613, temperature 1.0), and the agent works under domain policy and tools. Success means the final database matches the ground truth and the required outputs are present. The paper defines pass^k = E_task[C(c,k)/C(n,k)], "the chance that all k i.i.d. task trials are successful". The abstract says gpt-4o-class agents "succeed on <50% of the tasks" and that pass^8 is "<25% in retail". The blog says it "drops to ~25% on pass^8 in τ-retail".
- The paper itself notes that reward 1 is "necessary but not always sufficient". Its example is a return issued without the user's explicit confirmation, which breaks policy. That is the gap Grapim's clause-level judge covers.
- Change: in `risk.mjs` `judge()`, stop re-asking only broken probes. For high-severity rules, ask every probe k times and report pass^k per rule. Under pass^k, a 1-of-3 break is a failure, not "kept, unstable". Keep the record of which tries broke.

**1b. tau2-Bench: Evaluating Conversational Agents in a Dual-Control Environment.** Victor Barres, Honghua Dong, Soham Ray, Xujie Si, Karthik Narasimhan, arXiv 9 Jun 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07982
- Finding: it adds a telecom domain in which **the user also has tools** and shares state with the agent. It includes a task generator and a user simulator bound to tools and observable state. The abstract reports "significant performance drops" from no-user to dual-control settings (no figure in the abstract).
- Change: for agents that guide customers through steps (reset, verify, upload), a probe should check whether the agent told the customer to do the right thing, not only what it said. This matters later, for telecom and fintech onboarding flows.

**2. Red Teaming Language Models with Language Models.** Ethan Perez, Saffron Huang, Francis Song, Trevor Cai, Roman Ring, John Aslanides, Amelia Glaese, Nat McAleese, Geoffrey Irving (DeepMind), arXiv 7 Feb 2022. https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.03286
- Finding: test cases generated by an LM, scored by a classifier, uncovered "tens of thousands of offensive replies" from a 280B chatbot. The paper compares zero-shot, few-shot, supervised and RL generation, which trade diversity against difficulty. It also reports harms that only appear over multi-turn dialogue. Its conclusion is that the method is promising "but not sufficient".
- Change: this is the core citation for "an LLM writes the adversarial messages". In `risk.mjs`, feed probes that broke the agent back in as few-shot examples for the next round of generation, the paper's few-shot step. Also report diversity, for example near-duplicate messages per rule, so that 3 probes are not 3 rephrasings of one probe.

**3. Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena.** Lianmin Zheng, Wei-Lin Chiang, Ying Sheng, Siyuan Zhuang, Zhanghao Wu, Yonghao Zhuang, Zi Lin, Zhuohan Li, Dacheng Li, Eric P. Xing, Hao Zhang, Joseph E. Gonzalez, Ion Stoica, arXiv 9 Jun 2023, NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks. https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685
- Finding: on MT-bench, first turn (Tables 5a/5b), GPT-4 pairwise vs humans agrees 66% with ties and 85% without. Human vs human is 63% and 81%. The paper names position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias, plus limited reasoning on math. GPT-4's position consistency is 65.0% with the default prompt and 77.5% with few-shot. On its 20 math questions, a **reference-guided** judge cuts failures from 14/20 to 3/20, and chain of thought cuts them to 6/20.
- Change: giving the judge the exact clause is effectively a reference-guided judge, and that part of the design is well supported. Two changes follow. Verbosity bias suggests the `keyword_check` hint in `risk.mjs` could anchor the judge, so measure verdicts with and without it on overridden cases. And never use 80%+ as a claim about Grapim's own judge. Measure that judge instead (see Shankar and Thakur below).

**4. Great, Now Write an Article About That: The Crescendo Multi-Turn LLM Jailbreak Attack.** Mark Russinovich, Ahmed Salem, Ronen Eldan (Microsoft), arXiv 2 Apr 2024 (v3 26 Feb 2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01833
- Finding: the attack opens with harmless questions and escalates by referring back to the model's own replies. It reports high attack success on ChatGPT, Gemini Pro and Ultra, LLaMA-2/3 70b and Anthropic Chat. The automated version (Crescendomation) beats other state-of-the-art jailbreaks on an AdvBench subset by 29-61% on GPT-4 and 49-71% on Gemini-Pro.
- Change: single-turn probes in `risk.mjs` cannot catch a customer who gets an exception step by step ("you said you could help with fees..." then "so waive this one"). Add multi-turn escalation probes for high-severity rules.

## Further work that matters for Grapim

**5. LLMs Get Lost In Multi-Turn Conversation.** Philippe Laban, Hiroaki Hayashi, Yingbo Zhou, Jennifer Neville (Microsoft / Salesforce), arXiv 9 May 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120
- Finding: top models do worse in multi-turn than single-turn conversations, with "an average drop of 39% across six generation tasks" across more than 200,000 simulated conversations. Most of the drop is "a significant increase in unreliability", not lost ability.
- Change: this is the second independent reason (with Crescendo) to add multi-turn probes. It is also a reason for `live.mjs` to accept and judge earlier turns. Today it sees only the last message and answer, so it misses a violation set up two turns earlier.

**6. Who Validates the Validators? Aligning LLM-Assisted Evaluation of LLM Outputs with Human Preferences.** Shreya Shankar, J.D. Zamfirescu-Pereira, Björn Hartmann, Aditya G. Parameswaran, Ian Arawjo, arXiv 18 Apr 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272
- Finding: EvalGen asks people to grade a subset of outputs and uses those grades to pick the evaluator that best matches them. It identifies "criteria drift": grading outputs changes the grader's criteria, so evaluation cannot be fully specified before looking at outputs.
- Change: overrides in `risk.mjs` (`setOverride`) are exactly this human-graded subset. Use them per deployment. Show judge-vs-person agreement, and feed the override notes back to the judge as few-shot examples for that client's rules. Overrides are the client's criteria drift, recorded.

**7. Judging the Judges: Evaluating Alignment and Vulnerabilities in LLMs-as-Judges.** Aman Singh Thakur, Kartik Choudhary, Venkat Srinik Ramayapally, Sankaran Vaidyanathan, Dieuwke Hupkes, arXiv 18 Jun 2024, GEM2 workshop 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12624
- Finding: across 13 judges, only the largest reach reasonable human alignment, and they still fall "well short" of inter-human agreement. Judges are sensitive to prompt complexity and length and lean toward leniency. The authors "rediscover the importance of using alignment metrics beyond simple percent alignment". Which chance-corrected metric they used (Scott's pi or Cohen's kappa) is **(unverified, not in the abstract)**.
- Change: report agreement as Cohen's kappa plus the full 2x2 table (judge kept / person broke, and the reverse), not percent agreement. Most probes are kept, so percent agreement looks high by chance. Leniency also means the dangerous error is a false "kept". In `live.mjs`, one prompt holding up to 40 rules is the long, complex prompt the paper warns about. Send each rule separately, or pre-select the relevant rules, and compare against overrides.

**8. Replacing Judges with Juries: Evaluating LLM Generations with a Panel of Diverse Models.** Pat Verga, Sebastian Hofstätter, Sophia Althammer, Yixuan Su, Aleksandra Piktus, Arkady Arkhangorodsky, Minjie Xu, Naomi White, Patrick Lewis (Cohere), arXiv 29 Apr 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796
- Finding: a panel of smaller judges from different model families beats a single GPT-4 judge across 3 judge settings and 6 datasets. It shows less bias toward its own model family and costs "over seven times less".
- Change: Grapim's 3-try vote re-samples **both the agent and the same judge model**, which mixes up agent instability with judge noise. Split the two. Re-ask the agent for pass^k (paper 1), and when the verdict is close, vote with judges from 2-3 different model families on the **same** answer. This is also a sales point: "judged by a panel, not by the model that wrote the agent".

**9. Can LLMs Follow Simple Rules? (RuLES).** Norman Mu, Sarah Chen, Zifan Wang, Sizhe Chen, David Karamardian, Lulwa Aljeraisy, Basel Alomair, Dan Hendrycks, David Wagner, arXiv 6 Nov 2023. https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04235
- Finding: across 14 scenarios with programmatic checks, "almost all current models struggle to follow scenario rules", even on straightforward cases. Simple optimization attacks raise failure rates further. The abstract gives no percentages.
- Change: it supports the premise that written rules are broken often. It also argues for adding a **deterministic check** wherever a rule is mechanical (an amount cap, a required disclosure, no refund above X in `actions`). Let code, not the LLM, decide those in `risk.mjs`, and keep the LLM for judgment calls. Today's `phraseCheck` is only a hint.

**10. Identifying the Risks of LM Agents with an LM-Emulated Sandbox (ToolEmu).** Yangjun Ruan, Honghua Dong, Andrew Wang, Silviu Pitis, Yongchao Zhou, Jimmy Ba, Yann Dubois, Chris J. Maddison, Tatsunori Hashimoto, arXiv 25 Sep 2023 (ICLR 2024 per the authors **(unverified on page)**). https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15817
- Finding: an LM emulates tools and an LM safety evaluator flags risky actions. In human evaluation, "68.8%" of the failures it found were valid real-world failures. The benchmark has 36 tools and 144 cases, and the safest agent tested still fails "23.9%" of the time.
- Change: (a) about 1 in 3 failures flagged by an LM safety evaluator were not valid in their study. Grapim's "broken" verdicts need the override and agreement loop before being shown to clients as certain. (b) For agents whose tools can't be called safely in a test, Grapim could emulate tool responses rather than skip action checks.

**11. ST-WebAgentBench: A Benchmark for Evaluating Safety and Trustworthiness in Web Agents.** Ido Levy, Ben Wiesel, Sami Marreed, Alon Oved, Avi Yaeli, Nir Mashkif, Segev Shlomov (IBM), arXiv 9 Oct 2024, ICLR 2026 per the arXiv page. https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06703
- Finding: 222 enterprise tasks paired with policies. Completion under Policy (CuP) gives credit only when the task is done **and** every policy is respected. Average CuP is "less than two-thirds" of the agents' nominal completion rate.
- Change: Grapim reports only kept/broken. It should also catch the opposite failure, where an agent keeps the rule by refusing everything, by having the judge note whether the customer's legitimate need was met. Otherwise a fix in `fix.mjs` that makes the agent refuse more looks like a pure improvement.

**12. AgentDojo: A Dynamic Environment to Evaluate Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses for LLM Agents.** Edoardo Debenedetti, Jie Zhang, Mislav Balunović, Luca Beurer-Kellner, Marc Fischer, Florian Tramèr (ETH Zurich), arXiv 19 Jun 2024 (NeurIPS 2024 Datasets and Benchmarks **(unverified on page)**). https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13352
- Finding: 97 tasks (email, e-banking, travel) and 629 security test cases. State-of-the-art LLMs fail many tasks even without an attack, and existing injection attacks break some security properties but not all.
- Change: Grapim's probes attack only through the customer's message. Agents that read order notes, emails or knowledge-base pages can also be pushed off policy by **text inside tool results**. Later, add an injection probe type where the adversarial text sits in a tool result. Not urgent for plain support bots.

**13. SOP-Bench: Complex Industrial SOPs for Evaluating LLM Agents.** Subhrangshu Nandi, Arghya Datta, et al. (24 authors, Amazon), arXiv 9 Jun 2025, v2 23 Feb 2026, KDD 2026 per the search result **(unverified on arXiv page)**. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08119
- Finding: more than 2,000 tasks from expert-written SOPs across 12 business domains. The SOPs are written by people, and the tools, APIs and datasets are AI-generated and human-validated. v2 reports Claude 4 Opus at 72.4% and Claude 4.5 Sonnet at 63.3% on ReAct tasks, with the best result ranging from 57% to 100% by domain. (v1 reported averages of about 27% for function-calling agents and 48% for ReAct, and incorrect tool choice nearly 100% of the time when the tool registry is oversized, per the v1 abstract via search; not re-opened.)
- Change: this is the closest published work to "tests from a company's own written procedures". It keeps a **human check of the generated artifacts**. Grapim already has the analogue (the cases draft the engineer approves). Make the rule list itself reviewable before probes are spent, since a misread clause wastes every probe written for it.

## Not found

I found no peer-reviewed paper that does exactly Grapim's pipeline: policy document, then extracted clauses, then per-clause adversarial probes, then clause-cited verdicts, then a fix and re-test. tau-bench, ST-WebAgentBench and SOP-Bench hand-build or co-build the policies and tasks. That gap is a fair claim in a pitch, but phrase it as "we did not find", not "nobody has".

## Ranked upgrades

1. **Judge-vs-person agreement from overrides (S).** Add a count over `overrides.json` plus scan records (`judgeStatus` vs `status`): Cohen's kappa, the 2x2 table, and false-kept vs false-broken. Add a small random sample of *kept* answers to the review queue, because today people only see what the judge flagged, so false "kept" verdicts are never measured. Basis: Shankar 2024, Thakur 2024, ToolEmu 2023.
2. **pass^k per rule, symmetric sampling (S-M).** In `risk.mjs` `judge()`, for high-severity rules ask every probe k=3 times, not only broken ones. Report pass^3, and treat any broken try as a finding (shown as "broke 1 of 3"), not a majority-kept pass. Basis: tau-bench 2024.
3. **Multi-turn escalation probes (M).** A simulated customer (an LLM with a hidden goal tied to one clause, as in tau-bench) runs 3-5 turns of Crescendo-style pressure. The judge rules on the whole transcript. Use it for high-severity rules first. Basis: Crescendo 2024, Laban 2025, tau-bench 2024, Perez 2022.
4. **Separate agent variance from judge variance; jury on close calls (M).** Re-asking the agent measures reliability (upgrade 2). When a verdict is unstable or high-severity broken, judges from 2-3 model families rule on the same answer. Basis: Verga 2024, Zheng 2023.
5. **Held-out re-test plus over-refusal check after a fix (S-M).** In `fix.mjs` / `recheck`, also run fresh probes the fix never saw, and have the judge record whether the legitimate request was still served. Report "fixed without new refusals", not just fewer broken. Basis: Perez 2022 (generated tests as a finite sample), ST-WebAgentBench CuP.
6. **Live judging with history and per-rule calls (M).** Have `live.mjs` accept the earlier turns. Judge each relevant rule on its own, or pre-filter the rules, instead of putting 40 rules in one prompt. Check it against overrides. Basis: Laban 2025, Thakur 2024.

Later, not ranked: deterministic checks for mechanical rules (RuLES), tool-result injection probes (AgentDojo), emulated tools for agents that can't be called safely (ToolEmu).
